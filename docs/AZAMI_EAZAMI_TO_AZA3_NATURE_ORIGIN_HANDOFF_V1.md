# From Azami and EAzami to aza3 — origin of the Nature-scale question

**Status:** conceptual handoff / research-origin record  
**Date:** 2026-10-04  
**Role:** preserve why aza3 changed from a narrower Chapter-3 follow-up into a test of phenotypic evolvability in a young *Cirsium* radiation.

---

# 1. The question did not begin with evolvability

The original programme did not start by asking why thistles are unusually evolvable.

It began with a much more concrete natural-history problem:

> Why are *Cirsium* capitula so phenotypically diverse, and how is that diversity associated with environment?

The first phase therefore focused on **phenotype space and geography**, not on genomes or adaptive radiation.

---

# 2. Azami — first discovery: the capitulum is not one ecological trait

Azami assembled a global image-derived capitulum phenotype atlas.

The current numerical surface contains:

- 46,276 observations;
- 259 taxa;
- 22 measured endpoints;
- 11 biologically named constructs after biological reaggregation.

The measured phenotype space includes, among other axes:

- presentation/orientation;
- floral lightness, chroma and hue;
- head elongation and compactness;
- involucre form;
- bract projection / armature;
- surface traits.

The strongest spatial results were not a single whole-capitulum syndrome.

Two headline associations survived biological reaggregation, sampling-composition sensitivity, spatial/residual tests and all 52 historical-placement trees:

1. **floral chroma × short-wave radiation**
   - beta = -0.345372 in the construct-level atlas;
   - full-chain spatial beta = -0.712411;
2. **presentation angle × annual precipitation**
   - beta = +0.304359 in the construct-level atlas;
   - full-chain spatial beta = +0.286086.

Other colour/hue and architectural associations exist, but many do not survive the same complete sensitivity chain.

## What Azami changed conceptually

The first important lesson was not “colour responds to radiation” or “orientation responds to precipitation.”

It was:

> **Different parts of the same capitulum appear to occupy different ecological axes.**

The capitulum therefore began to look less like one floral syndrome and more like a **multidimensional reproductive phenotype**.

But Azami remained spatial and observational.

It could show where phenotype diversity occurs and which environmental axes covary with it.

It could not tell how that diversity was assembled through evolutionary time.

---

# 3. EAzami — the original goal was historical depth

EAzami was developed to add the time axis.

The initial question was approximately:

> When did the different capitulum components change, were they transformed together, and can their historical environmental triggers be identified?

A young Japanese *Cirsium* radiation provided the test.

The accepted synthesis places 36 of 38 sampled Japanese taxon concepts in one dominant young radiation.

Three source-backed discrete capitulum components could be analysed across the topology ensemble:

- orientation;
- phyllary posture;
- involucre stickiness.

The historical results were:

- orientation: **4–6** minimum state changes across 1,000 UFBoot topologies;
- phyllary posture: **exactly 3**;
- stickiness: **exactly 5**.

Their changes also occupied unequal evolutionary depths:

- phyllary deeper-permissive than stickiness: 1000/1000 paired topologies;
- phyllary deeper-permissive than orientation: 993/1000;
- orientation deeper-permissive than stickiness: 905/1000;
- full central ordering: 898/1000.

And critically:

- **0/3 trait pairs** passed the robust shared-transition-localization rule.

## The unexpected result

EAzami had been designed to ask **how deep in time** trait differentiation occurred.

Instead, it exposed the opposite puzzle:

> **The radiation is young, yet the same reproductive head has already been repeatedly rebuilt in multiple, partly independent ways.**

The biologically interesting fact was no longer simply the depth ordering.

It was the density of phenotypic change inside a shallow radiation.

The project therefore moved from:

> Which trait changed first?

to:

> **How could so much phenotypic change happen so quickly?**

---

# 4. The historical-trigger route did not solve the puzzle

Orientation was the best-resolved ecological bridge.

Under the frozen transition estimator, U→D transitions align with a present-day two-axis regime:

- BIO15 up;
- BIO1 down.

The exact finite-map ranks were:

- n>=5: 16/792 = 2.02%;
- n>=3: 19/1716 = 1.11%;
- strict n>=10: 4/126 = 3.17%;
- bidirectional floor: 3/126 = 2.38%.

This showed that at least one repeated capitulum component is ecologically structured.

But projecting the same regime backward did not identify the origin trigger.

For the sole orientation event with bounded chronology and palaeolocation:

- 94 chronology pairs × four regions = 376 scenarios;
- the current BIO15-up/BIO1-down regime matched only **99/376 = 26.3%**;
- only 6/94 chronologies matched in all four regions;
- at the central 0.79–0.74 Ma chronology, BIO15 moved in the opposite direction in all four regions.

Broader historical diagnostics also failed to identify one recurring coarse trigger:

- 17 BIOCLIM variables × six dated lineage contexts;
- 15,472 scenario × variable combinations;
- **0/324** robust event-level climate classes;
- **0/21** robust global sea-level event-metric classes.

## Why this mattered

This was initially experienced as a causal ceiling.

But it changed the question again.

If one shared historical environmental pulse does not explain repeated capitulum change, then perhaps the central mystery is not:

> What external event repeatedly triggered change?

but:

> **Why was this lineage capable of generating so many new capitulum states whenever ecological opportunity or selection acted?**

The focus therefore moved from **trigger** to **capacity for change**.

---

# 5. Functional synthesis clarified the problem but did not solve it

The literature/meta-analysis programme showed that capitulum components can plausibly affect different ecological interfaces.

Current working assignments are:

- orientation → abiotic reproductive exposure / timing;
- phyllary architecture → mechanical antagonist access;
- stickiness → context-dependent arthropod-community filtering.

Across four independent *Cirsium* reproductive-herbivory programmes, reducing insect herbivory increases viable/mature seed output by:

- RR = **2.674**;
- 95% CI = **2.388–2.993**.

Other meta-syntheses show:

- no universal pollinator-versus-antagonist dominance;
- pollinator dependence is common but is not equivalent to current pollen limitation;
- demographic transmission of seed effects is context dependent;
- generic literature meta-analysis has reached its decision ceiling for the current doctoral hypotheses.

These results make the phenotype biologically consequential.

But they still do not explain why the radiation can repeatedly generate new trait combinations.

This distinction is central:

> **Function explains why a phenotype may be selected. It does not explain where the capacity to repeatedly generate that phenotype comes from.**

---

# 6. The new Nature-scale question

The programme therefore arrives at a different question from the one with which Azami began:

> ## **Why can this young thistle radiation repeatedly generate so many different capitulum phenotypes in such little evolutionary time?**

Or more generally:

> ## **Where does the capacity for rapid phenotypic innovation come from?**

The current Nature-scale hypothesis is not that one capitulum trait has one adaptive function.

It is that rapid phenotypic radiation may be fuelled by **reuse and recombination of pre-existing genomic variation**.

A strong working formulation is:

> **Rapid capitulum diversification was fuelled not primarily by repeated de novo invention, but by recurrent reuse, sorting and exchange of ancestral genomic variants across a reticulation-prone radiation.**

This is a hypothesis to be tested, not a conclusion inherited from Chapter 1 or Chapter 2.

---

# 7. Competing genomic explanations

aza3 should not be designed to “find the gene for” orientation, colour, phyllaries or stickiness.

It should discriminate among competing sources of phenotypic innovation.

## G1 — repeated de novo mutation

Each phenotypic transition is produced by a new mutation or new regulatory solution.

Prediction:

- independent phenotype transitions usually have different causal haplotypes;
- local genealogies follow the species/population history except at independently mutated sites;
- allele ages are compatible with recent lineage-specific origin.

## G2 — ancestral standing variation

Trait-associated variants predate the radiation and are repeatedly sorted into descendant populations.

Prediction:

- the same or homologous old haplotypes recur across otherwise separated lineages;
- trait-associated allele ages predate focal species splits;
- phenotypic transitions can occur without corresponding genome-wide ancestry convergence.

## G3 — introgressive reuse

Useful trait-associated haplotypes move between lineages after divergence.

Prediction:

- phenotype-associated genomic regions show local genealogy discordant with the genome-wide background;
- local ancestry / network tests support gene flow involving the shared state;
- the reused block is more similar among phenotype-sharing populations than expected under the species history.

## G4 — polyploid/homeolog/regulatory reuse

Genome duplication, homeolog sorting or regulatory reweighting releases alternative phenotypic states without requiring new coding innovation.

Prediction:

- phenotype differences depend on cytotype/homeolog dosage or allele-specific expression;
- the same biochemical/developmental toolkit is retained across states;
- repeated phenotype shifts can arise through regulatory switching in different genomic backgrounds.

No one model is assumed to dominate all capitulum modules.

---

# 8. Why the existing results make these hypotheses testable

Several existing observations make the genomic-reuse hypothesis plausible enough to test but not already established.

### A. Rapid radiation

The dominant Japanese radiation contains 36/38 sampled Japanese concepts in the current broad nuclear synthesis.

This gives a shallow time window in which large capitulum disparity accumulated.

### B. Historical mosaicism

Orientation, phyllary posture and stickiness changed repeatedly but not synchronously.

This creates repeated phenotypic events whose genomic origins can be compared.

### C. Species-tip compression

Four of four reviewed polymorphic systems lose state multiplicity when compressed to a single species-tip state.

At least one morph-linked system increases the minimum transition count when population/morph samples are retained.

This indicates that species-level coding is hiding recent evolutionary variation.

### D. Reticulation / ILS already exist

Published phylogenomics reports substantial gene-tree discordance and reticulation-compatible histories in East Asian *Cirsium*.

Therefore a purely bifurcating, independent-origin model should not be assumed.

### E. Cytotype and genome-size variation already exist

Diploid, tetraploid and hexaploid states occur inside the broader Japanese radiation context, and East-Asian studies report chromosome/genome-size change.

Ploidy does not deterministically map to orientation, but it remains a candidate source of genomic evolvability.

### F. Genomic resources now exist

A reusable East-Asian *Cirsium* nuclear reference genome exists for *C. nipponicum*, alongside public target-capture and phylotranscriptomic data.

The programme is therefore no longer blocked at “no genomic resource exists.”

# 9. How the previous projects become a single causal programme

The projects are not merged as one paper.

Their roles are different.

## Azami — phenotype space

Question:

> What dimensions of capitulum diversity exist, and how are they distributed across environmental space?

Contribution:

- global breadth;
- continuous phenotype axes;
- multiple ecological associations;
- evidence that the capitulum does not collapse to one simple ecological syndrome.

## EAzami — evolutionary history

Question:

> How was capitulum diversity assembled through a young radiation?

Contribution:

- repeated state changes;
- unequal evolutionary depth;
- unsynchronized transition localization;
- discovery that large phenotypic reassembly occurred in little evolutionary time.

## aza3 — source of evolvability

Question:

> **What genomic processes made repeated phenotypic reassembly possible?**

Primary new evidence:

- same-individual phenotype + nuclear ancestry + cytotype;
- population-level sampling of repeated/polymorphic transitions;
- local genealogies and ancestry;
- candidate-region / regulatory follow-up where justified;
- floral expression / pigment chemistry for selected mechanistically tractable traits;
- separate focal fitness experiments where adaptation is explicitly tested.

The conceptual progression is therefore:

`phenotype space → repeated evolutionary history → genomic source of evolvability → fitness consequence`

---

# 10. Why colour and orientation play different roles

Not every capitulum module needs to carry the whole programme.

## Colour — molecular flagship

Colour is the strongest candidate for closing:

`genotype → regulation → pigment → optical phenotype → environment`.

Azami already shows a robust chroma–radiation association.

Public resources recover flavonoid/anthocyanin pathway homologues, but current public data do not establish repeated causal regulatory reuse.

Therefore colour is well suited to test:

- shared versus independent causal loci;
- ancestral haplotype retention;
- introgression;
- cis/trans regulatory reuse;
- homeolog-specific expression where relevant.

## Orientation — ecological causal flagship

Orientation has the strongest current transition-level ecological prediction.

It is suited to close:

`trait → wetting/presentation mediator → reproductive process → filled achenes`.

Orientation therefore tests adaptive function more directly, while colour may provide the clearest route to genomic mechanism.

This division is intentional.

A Nature-scale programme does not require one trait to solve phenotype, genome, ecology and fitness simultaneously.

---

# 11. The high-risk / high-reward predictions

The programme becomes genuinely informative only if the hypotheses can fail.

### Prediction 1 — repeated phenotypic states reuse old genomic material

If repeated states are usually caused by independent recent variants, the standing-variation/reuse hypothesis loses.

### Prediction 2 — local genomic history predicts phenotype better than the species tree for at least some repeated states

If trait-associated regions always follow the genome-wide bifurcating history, introgressive reuse loses.

### Prediction 3 — standing variation / reticulation / cytotype diversity predicts capacity to generate phenotype combinations

If high genomic discordance/variation has no relationship to trait reassembly after age and sampling controls, the proposed evolvability link weakens.

### Prediction 4 — genomic reuse generates combinatorial rather than whole-head inheritance

If the same genomic histories always move all capitulum modules together, the reconfigurable-toolkit model loses.

### Prediction 5 — at least selected reused phenotypes have demonstrable fitness consequences

If focal manipulations show no ecologically relevant mediator or reproductive-fitness effect, genomic reuse may explain phenotype production but not adaptive radiation.

---

# 12. What would make this Nature-scale rather than another Cirsium paper?

The target is **not**:

- a better *Cirsium* tree;
- a RAD-seq population structure paper;
- a GWAS for flower colour;
- an orientation manipulation paper;
- another trait–climate association;
- proof that one head trait is adaptive.

The target is a general evolutionary statement:

> **Rapid phenotypic innovation can arise because a young radiation repeatedly reuses and recombines pre-existing genomic solutions rather than inventing each phenotype de novo.**

For a stronger version:

> **Reticulation and ancestral variation create a reusable genomic toolkit that increases the combinatorial evolvability of a complex reproductive organ.**

To support that statement, aza3 must link:

1. repeated phenotype;
2. population genomic history;
3. source/age of trait-associated variation;
4. module-specific reuse;
5. at least representative functional/fitness consequences.

A single association at any one of these layers is insufficient.

---

# 13. Why Cirsium is a useful test rather than merely the study organism

*Cirsium* is useful because several unusual properties coincide:

- a large and phenotypically diverse genus;
- a dominant young Japanese radiation;
- repeated capitulum-state changes inside that radiation;
- incomplete lineage sorting / reticulation-compatible history;
- cytotype/genome-size variation;
- multiple capitulum components with different plausible ecological roles;
- public global phenotype data from Azami;
- existing nuclear phylogenomic and transcriptomic resources;
- the possibility of sampling recent polymorphism before species-tip compression erases it.

The value of the system is therefore not simply that thistles have variable flowers.

It is that **rapid phenotype generation, reticulate genomic history and a complex modular reproductive structure coexist on a shallow timescale.**

---

# 14. What remains deliberately unresolved

This handoff does not assert:

- that the Japanese *Cirsium* radiation is already demonstrated to be an adaptive radiation;
- that reticulation caused its species richness;
- that standing variation caused the repeated capitulum states;
- that the capitulum is genetically or developmentally modular;
- that ploidy increases evolvability;
- that colour, orientation, phyllaries and stickiness share one genomic architecture;
- that every repeated phenotype is adaptive.

Those are the bets aza3 must discriminate.

---

# 15. Immediate aza3 design consequence

The original aza3 v1 plan remains valuable, but its role changes.

RAD-seq/population ancestry is no longer justified mainly as:

> “improve the Chapter 2 tree.”

It is justified as:

> **capture the population-level genomic material that species-tip analyses erase, so that the source of repeated phenotypic innovation can be identified.**

Therefore the highest-value sampling units are not simply taxonomically representative species.

They are populations that discriminate among de novo mutation, ancestral standing variation, introgression and polyploid/regulatory reuse.

Priority characteristics include:

- repeated or polymorphic capitulum states;
- ancestry-matched phenotype contrasts;
- known/possible contact or introgression;
- cytotype variation;
- availability of the same phenotype in more than one lineage;
- feasibility of same-individual phenotype, DNA, cytotype and voucher linkage.

---

# 16. The programme-level question to preserve

If later analyses become complicated, return to this question:

> ## **Why could a young thistle radiation repeatedly generate so much reproductive-phenotype diversity in so little evolutionary time?**

And the current highest-risk answer is:

> **Because the radiation inherited, exchanged and recombined a reusable genomic toolkit, allowing capitulum components to be repeatedly reassembled instead of reinvented de novo.**

Everything in aza3 should either increase or decrease confidence in that explanation.

If a planned analysis does neither, it is probably peripheral to the Nature-scale programme.
